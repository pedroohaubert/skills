# Reasoning

The thinking that comes before any pixel and stays behind every decision. Two ideas, one block: start from what the thing means, and turn every choice into a reason you can say. A screen with no meaning behind it is makeup, and a choice with no reason behind it is a guess. AI makes polished guesses cheap: ten good-looking screens before the coffee is done, with the same amount of thinking as one. The reasoning is what picks between them.

## Start with meaning, not pixels

For a new product, page, or flow, write four answers before the first layout. A change to an existing screen inherits them from the app.

1. **Who it is for.** Who the users are and what describes them: role, age, income, how often they come, what they know. When you can't write it, you don't know the problem yet: research it, or ask the user.
2. **What it means to them.** Status, help, guidance, trust, speed. The meaning decides what the screen must feel like and what it must put first.
3. **What came before.** The history of the thing and the conventions people already know. Conventions exist for a reason; reinvent only what the job needs (`precedents.md`).
4. **What competitors do.** The closest products, how they look, and what they charge, so you know which rules to keep and which to break. When the field is unknown, run a deep research first: the closest competitors, their screens, when they launched, how they make money.

Research is input, not the answer. After it, think: name the decisions that are still abstract and shape the form around them. Form follows function.

The urge to skip this is strong, because an hour of layout produces a screen and an hour of thinking produces nothing to show. Write the four answers anyway, in the plan or the reply, before the first layout.

**References come second.** Moodboards and galleries answer "how should this feel?", never "what is this?". Collect them after the meaning is written, and pick each for a reason. Take every reference apart: name the problem that screen was solving and check it is yours. Usually it isn't; then borrow only the parts that answer your job.

> Example: a learning site for designers. Who: designers who want to get better. Meaning: a gift back to the community, so the page puts the help first and the art second. Every choice on the page came from that meaning; none came from a moodboard.

## Taste is a decision with a reason

A design starts as a feeling: something looks right or off. The feeling is real and often the start of good work, but it becomes taste only once you can say why. A feeling can't be shared, defended, or repeated, and a teammate or an agent can't act on "make it feel nicer".

- **Every decision carries a "because" tied to the people or the job.** "Bigger, because it's the one thing people come here for" is a decision. "It looks nicer bigger" is still a feeling: go back, find the cause, and name it.
- **The reason picks between versions.** When several directions or variants exist, choose by which one best serves the meaning written above, and say so.
- **Reasons go in the reply.** When you show a screen, give the reason behind each choice that is not obvious, in one line each.
- **A feeling from the user is a cause to find.** When the user says "it's ugly" or "something's off", find and name the reason before changing anything (`composition.md`, "Reading the reviewer").
- **Taste is mostly subtraction.** Less, but better: everything that stays has to earn its spot with a job, and every removal is a decision. The working rule is "Subtract before adding" in `composition.md`.

Done when the four answers are written for new work and every non-obvious choice in the reply has its reason.
